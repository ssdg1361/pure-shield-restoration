## Century Village is its own kind of job

There are thousands of units in Century Village, most of them built in the 1970s, most of them stacked two or three high with shared plumbing running between them. That combination produces one failure over and over: a supply line, water heater or drain lets go upstairs, and the damage lands in the unit below.

What makes it a different job from a house is not the water. It's the paperwork and the access. There are usually two insurers involved, sometimes three parties if the association's policy touches it, and a set of building rules about when crews can work, where equipment can sit and how the common areas have to be protected. None of that changes what needs to happen to the wet drywall. It just means the documentation has to be right from the first hour, because everyone downstream is going to ask for it.

We handle these buildings regularly. We follow the association's protocol, coordinate access with the property manager when the owner is away, and photograph and meter everything as we go so the claim isn't argued from memory six weeks later.

## The half-year problem

A large share of Deerfield lives here part of the year. That single fact changes the shape of almost every job we do in this city.

A leak in an occupied home gets caught in hours. Somebody hears it, smells it, or steps in it. The same leak in a unit closed up from May to November runs for months. The water isn't worse. The time is.

By the time someone opens the door in the fall, drywall that took on water in June has been sitting in a warm, humid, closed box all summer. That is close to ideal conditions for mold, and it's why the fall calls are so often bigger jobs than the callers expected.

If you close up a place here for the season, the checks worth doing before you leave and the order worth checking things in when you get back are written up here: [what to check before you unpack](/blog/snowbird-return-home-checklist-florida/).

## Kitchens hide leaks better than anywhere else in the house

The most common mold job we do in Deerfield is a kitchen, and it's almost always the same story. A supply line or a drain behind the cabinet run seeps slowly. Nothing drips onto the floor. Nothing pools. The water just tracks along the base of the wall behind the lower cabinets, where nobody can see it, and feeds growth in the cavity.

The tells are subtle: a musty smell that's strongest when you open the cabinet under the sink, a toe kick that's swollen or separating, a section of baseboard that's discolored at the bottom edge.

Getting at it means opening the wall behind the counter, not cleaning what's visible on the surface. [Here's one of those jobs in Deerfield](/work/deerfield-kitchen-mold/), start to finish, with what was behind the cabinets.

## Bathrooms, attics, garages

### Bathrooms

Dark grout and discolored caulk are usually surface conditions. What matters is whether water has gotten behind the tile — under a shower pan, around a tub surround, into the wall the vanity sits against. A surface clean and a structural remediation are very different jobs at very different prices, and you can't tell which one you have from the outside.

### Attics and air handlers

If the air handler is in the attic or a closet, the condensate line is the thing to watch. A clogged line backs the pan up, and the water goes into the ceiling below. Attic mold in South Florida is almost never a roof leak — it's usually the AC, and the fix is as much about the drain line as the drywall.

### Garages

Poor door seals, a slab that wicks, and a second refrigerator running in the corner. Garages in Broward stay damper than people assume, and mold on the back wall or along the bottom of stored boxes is common and entirely fixable.

## Testing, estimates, and who does which

We get asked for mold testing a lot, so it's worth being direct about how this works in Florida.

Mold **assessment** — the testing, the air and surface samples, the written protocol — is a separate license from mold **remediation**, and in most cases one company can't do both on the same property. That's deliberate. It removes the incentive for the company finding the mold to also be the company selling the removal.

We're remediators. We hold Florida mold remediation license MRSR5407. When a job needs independent testing or a clearance sample, we work alongside a third-party assessor, which keeps those two things at arm's length the way the statute intends. If you want the longer version, including how to check any Florida license in about a minute, it's here: [mold inspection vs. mold remediation](/blog/mold-inspection-vs-mold-remediation-florida/).

An estimate is different from an assessment, and it's free. We come look, meter it, and tell you what the job actually is.

## Water mitigation, drying, and restoration

**Water mitigation** is the emergency half: [extracting standing water](/services/emergency-water-extraction/), stopping the spread, and getting the structure drying before damage compounds. That work happens in hours, not days.

**[Structural drying](/services/structural-drying/)** is the part people underestimate. Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings say the structure is dry, not until the surface feels dry. In this humidity that usually takes longer than anyone wants. Cutting it short is how a properly extracted flood turns into a mold job four months later.

**[Water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/)** is the full arc through to a space you can use again, and **[mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/)** is what's needed when growth has already established.

## The Cove, Deer Creek and the waterfront

Not all of Deerfield is condos. The single-family stock in Deer Creek and Crystal Lake fails differently — slab plumbing, aging water heaters, and AC condensate rather than shared risers.

On the Intracoastal side in The Cove, the recurring issue is wind-driven rain finding its way around sliders, window frames and door thresholds during storm season. That water enters at the perimeter and tracks into the wall assembly, so it often shows up as staining at the base of a wall well after the storm passed.
