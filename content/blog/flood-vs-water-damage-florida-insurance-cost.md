Water on the floor is water on the floor. But where it came from decides who pays for it, and most South Florida homeowners find that out at the worst possible moment.

## The short answer

**Flood water and water damage are two different things on your insurance policy.** A burst pipe or a failed water heater is sudden internal water, and a standard homeowners policy usually covers it. Water that rises from outside, from storm surge, heavy rain, or a canal that came up, is a flood, and standard homeowners policies generally exclude it. Flood is covered separately through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood policy. The cleanup work looks similar from the driveway, but the claim goes to a completely different place.

## Why outside water is handled differently

**Flood water is treated as contaminated from the moment it enters, no matter how clear it looks.** The industry standard that restoration companies work to, IICRC S500, sorts water into three categories. Category 1 is clean water from a supply line. Category 2 has some contamination, like a washing machine overflow. Category 3 is grossly contaminated, and ground surface water and storm surge land there by definition, because they pick up whatever is in the street, the soil, and the septic and sewer systems on the way to your house.

That classification is not a technicality. It changes the work:

- **Porous materials come out rather than getting dried.** Carpet, carpet pad, insulation, and drywall below the water line are removed and disposed of, because you cannot reliably disinfect the inside of them.
- **The area gets contained.** Sealed work zones and negative air keep contamination from traveling into the rest of the house on foot traffic and airflow.
- **Disinfecting is part of the job, not an add-on.** Surfaces that stay get cleaned and treated before anything is closed back up.
- **Drying is verified with meters, not by eye.** A wall can feel dry and still hold enough moisture for mold to start.

If a company treats storm surge like a clean water loss and just runs fans, you will be calling someone else in three weeks about mold.

## What actually drives the cost

**Nobody can quote a flood job honestly without seeing it, and the size of the puddle is not the main variable.** Four things move the number more than anything else:

- **How long the water sat.** The first day is the cheap day. Mold can start inside 24 to 48 hours in South Florida humidity, and every day after that moves material from the dry-and-save column to the remove-and-replace column.
- **How much has to be removed.** Cutting out two feet of drywall around a room is a different job from cutting out four feet and pulling the cabinets.
- **How far the water traveled.** Water moves under flooring and inside wall cavities. What looks like one wet room is often three, and moisture mapping is what finds that.
- **How long the structure takes to dry.** Most jobs run a few days of monitored drying. Our humidity stretches that out, so equipment stays until the readings say it is done rather than until the calendar says so.

Be wary of a firm price quoted over the phone before anyone has taken a reading. That number is either padded to cover the unknown or it is going to grow later.

## The Florida licensing thing worth knowing

**In Florida, the company that tests your home for mold generally cannot be the company that remediates it.** Under Florida Statute 468.8419, a licensed mold assessor may not perform remediation on a structure they assessed within the last twelve months, and a remediator may not perform an assessment on a structure they remediated in that window. The point is to remove the incentive to find more mold than is actually there.

## What to ask before you hire anyone

**The company that answers the phone at 2am is not automatically the right one.** Ask for the Florida license number and confirm it. Ask whether you will get moisture readings and photo documentation formatted for an adjuster, because a claim without documentation is a claim you argue about later. Ask who is actually on site, since with the large franchises the estimator you meet is often not the crew that shows up. And ask them to tell you what does not need doing, because a company that will not say no to any part of a job is selling, not assessing.

## If you have water in the house right now

Shut off the source if you can reach it safely, and stay out of standing water where electricity may be live. Photograph everything before anything moves, because that is what your adjuster wants. Then call someone.

When you do call your insurer, have four things ready. It makes the difference between a claim that moves and a claim that stalls.

- **When it started and what caused it.** Sudden versus gradual is the distinction the adjuster is listening for, and it decides your coverage.
- **Where the water came from.** Inside the house or in from outside. That is the flood question, and it decides which policy pays.
- **Photos and video from before anything was moved or torn out.** Take more than you think you need, including inside cabinets and under furniture.
- **Your policy number and whether you carry separate flood coverage.** If you are not sure, ask them directly rather than assuming.
